# 2.5 Kinds of Transactions

## Slide 1: Settlement Cycles – Cash, Tom, and Spot
*   **Cash Trades (T+0):** Settlement where payment and delivery occur on the same day as the trade.
*   **Tom Trades (T+1):** Settlement occurs on the next business day (Tomorrow) after the trade date.
*   **Spot Trades (T+2):** Settlement normally occurs two business days after the trade date; this is the standard for the Indian equity market.
*   **Market Nuances:** While T+1 is becoming the norm for Indian exchanges, FX markets globally use Spot (T+2) as their default.

### Notes
In the securities market, the time between executing a trade and the actual exchange of money and securities is called the settlement cycle. "Cash" trades are the fastest, happening on the same day (T+0). While rare in complex financial markets due to the time needed for accounting, they are common in daily life when buying groceries. "Tom" trades settle on the next day (T+1), a cycle the Indian stock market has recently migrated toward for all stocks. Finally, "Spot" trades settle on T+2. It is important to remember that in global foreign exchange (FX) markets, when someone refers to a "Spot" price, they are usually talking about a transaction that will be completed in two business days.

## Slide 2: Forward Transactions – The Customized Contract
*   **Definition:** A direct agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a future date for a price decided today.
*   **OTC Nature:** These are Over-the-Counter (OTC) contracts, meaning they are not traded on a formal exchange.
*   **Customization:** Terms like quantity, quality, and delivery dates are tailored to the specific needs of the counterparties.
*   **Counterparty Risk:** Since there is no central guarantor, these contracts rely on mutual trust and carry the risk that one party might default.

### Notes
Forward contracts are the oldest type of derivative and are purely private agreements. Imagine a farmer agreeing to sell wheat to a miller six months from now at a price they fix today. This allows both parties to plan their finances without worrying about future price swings. Because these are "Over-the-Counter" (OTC) transactions, every detail can be customized. However, the biggest downside is "counterparty risk." If the price of wheat skyrockets, the farmer might be tempted to back out; if it crashes, the miller might default. Because there is no exchange to guarantee the deal, forwards are generally only entered into by parties who know and trust each other.

## Slide 3: Futures – Standardized and Guaranteed
*   **Definition:** Standardized, exchange-traded versions of forward contracts.
*   **Standardization:** The exchange dictates the lot size, quality, and specific delivery/settlement dates.
*   **Clearing House Guarantee:** The Clearing Corporation acts as a counterparty to every trade, eliminating individual counterparty risk.
*   **Margin Requirements:** Participants must maintain upfront and mark-to-market margins to ensure financial integrity.

### Notes
Futures are essentially "Forwards 2.0." To solve the problems of forwards, stock exchanges created standardized contracts. You cannot customize a future; the exchange decides that one "lot" of wheat is 10 metric tons or one "lot" of Nifty is a specific number of units. The magic of futures lies in the Clearing Corporation. By stepping into the middle of every trade through "novation," they guarantee that you will get your money or your asset even if the person on the other side disappears. To protect themselves, the exchange forces everyone to pay "margins"—a form of security deposit that is adjusted daily based on market movements.

## Slide 4: Options – Rights Without Obligations
*   **Core Concept:** A contract that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a set price.
*   **Option Premium:** The price paid by the buyer to the seller (writer) to acquire this right.
*   **Strike Price:** The pre-determined price at which the asset can be bought or sold.
*   **Expiration:** Options have a stated date after which the right expires and becomes worthless.

### Notes
Options are a unique financial tool because they offer "asymmetric risk." When you buy an option, you are buying a "right." If the market moves in your favor, you exercise that right and make a profit. If the market moves against you, you can simply choose to do nothing and let the option expire. The most you can lose is the "premium" you paid to the seller. The seller, or "writer," however, is in a different position—they pocket your premium today but are under a legal "obligation" to fulfill the contract if you choose to exercise your right, regardless of how much the market has moved against them.

## Slide 5: Call Options vs. Put Options
*   **Call Option:** Gives the buyer the right to **buy** the underlying asset; used when expecting prices to rise.
*   **Put Option:** Gives the buyer the right to **sell** the underlying asset; used when expecting prices to fall.
*   **In-the-Money (ITM):** When the market price makes exercising the option profitable.
*   **Out-of-the-Money (OTM):** When the market price makes the option worthless to exercise.

### Notes
To keep Call and Put options straight, think of their purpose. You buy a "Call" when you want to "call" an asset away from someone at a low price because you think it’s going higher. You buy a "Put" when you want to "put" (sell) an asset to someone at a high price because you think the market is crashing. If you buy a Call on Nifty at 11,000 and the market goes to 11,200, your option is "In-the-Money" because you can buy at 11,000 and immediately sell at the higher market price. If the market falls to 10,800, your option is "Out-of-the-Money," and you simply walk away, losing only the premium you paid.

## Slide 6: Swaps – Exchanging Future Cash Flows
*   **Definition:** A derivative contract where two parties exchange cash flows based on a pre-arranged formula.
*   **Common Use Case:** Interest Rate Swaps, where one party trades a "floating" rate for a "fixed" rate.
*   **Notional Amount:** The principal amount used to calculate interest, though this principal itself is rarely exchanged.
*   **Risk Management:** Used by corporates to manage volatility in interest rates, currencies, and commodity prices.

### Notes
A Swap is like a financial "trade" of obligations. The most common type is an Interest Rate Swap. Imagine a company has a loan with a floating interest rate that changes every quarter. This uncertainty makes budgeting difficult. To fix this, they enter a swap where they agree to pay a "fixed" rate to a dealer, and in exchange, the dealer pays them the "floating" rate. These two floating rates cancel each other out, effectively converting the company's unpredictable loan into a stable, fixed-rate payment. It’s a powerful way for businesses to "swap" a risk they don't want for a certainty they do.

## Slide 7: Trading vs. Speculating
*   **Trading:** Short-term activity (hours, days, weeks) based on price patterns and technical analysis.
*   **Speculating:** Betting on price changes over any period, often based on private views or fundamental shifts.
*   **The Probability Gap:** Traders generally have a higher probability of success as they rely on repeating patterns; speculators take higher-risk directional bets.
*   **Market Benefit:** Both activities are vital as they provide significant liquidity to the securities market.

### Notes
While often used interchangeably, "trading" and "speculating" have subtle differences. Trading is a disciplined, short-term activity where participants look for repeating patterns in charts to make quick profits. Speculating is more about taking a "view" on where a stock is going based on news or fundamental analysis. Speculators often take much higher risks and use more leverage, hoping for a massive payout if their "guess" is correct. Despite their different motives, the market needs both, as their constant buying and selling ensures that there is always someone available to take the other side of a trade.
