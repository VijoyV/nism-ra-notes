# Slide 1

## Defining Securities: Claims and Contracts
*   **Transferable Claims:** Financial contracts that can be moved from one holder to another.
*   **Two Core Forms:** Represent either **indebtedness** (debt/bonds) or **ownership interest** (equity shares).
*   **Issuer Base:** Created by business entities, financial institutions, or the government.
*   **Interchangeable Terms:** Also referred to as "Instruments" or "Products" within the industry.

## Notes
To understand the securities market, we must first define what a "security" is. At its simplest, a security is a transferable financial claim. If you hold one, it serves as legal evidence of either a promise to pay you back a debt—such as a bond or debenture—or a sign that you own a piece of a company, like an equity share. These are issued by entities that need to raise money, including private companies and the government. In professional finance circles, you will often hear these called "instruments" or "products," and they all essentially represent a way for an investor to convert their savings into a financial asset that provides a return.

# Slide 2

## The Bridge: Aligning Issuers and Investors
*   **Savings to Investment:** Provides a channel for moving idle resources to productive uses.
*   **Issuer Goal:** Raising long-term capital for business expansion.
*   **Investor Goal:** Investing savings into assets that provide returns.
*   **Exit Mechanism:** Allows investors to transfer their rights to others without impacting the issuer.

## Notes
The primary purpose of the securities market is to act as a bridge. On one side, we have savers with surplus funds; on the other, we have businesses and governments that need capital to grow. By issuing securities, these borrowers can raise money for the long term. However, most investors don't want their money locked away forever. The market solves this by providing an exit mechanism. An investor can sell their claim to another investor in the "secondary market." This alignment is crucial because it allows the business to keep the capital for a long duration while the investor enjoys the flexibility to exit whenever they choose.

# Slide 3

## Market Efficacy: Liquidity and Price Discovery
*   **Efficacy through Efficiency:** Movement of capital depends on a functioning market.
*   **Creating Liquidity:** Bringing many buyers and sellers together to ensure ease of trading.
*   **Market Pricing:** Facilitates buying and selling at prices close to the current market value.
*   **Continuous Conversion:** Effectively turns long-term claims into short-term liquid assets for the holder.

## Notes
For capital to move effectively, the market must be efficient. This efficiency is measured by "liquidity." A liquid market is one where there are enough buyers and sellers that you can trade your securities quickly and at a fair price. Without this active presence of a secondary market, a 10-year bond would be a very rigid investment. But because the market brings everyone together, that 10-year security is effectively converted into a liquid asset that the holder can sell at any time. This transformation of "long-term capital" into "short-term liquidity" for the investor is one of the most important functions of the modern financial system.

# Slide 4

## The Financial Market Ecosystem
*   **Investors:** Savers and providers of funds (Households, Foreigners, etc.).
*   **Borrowers:** Seekers of funds (Business firms, Government).
*   **Intermediaries:** The infrastructure providers facilitating funds and securities transfer.
*   **Regulators:** Bodies responsible for orderly development and rule enforcement.

## Notes
Broadly speaking, the financial market consists of four main participants. First are the Investors or "Providers of funds," which include households, companies, and even foreign entities. Second are the Borrowers or "Seekers of funds," like corporations needing to build factories or governments needing to build infrastructure. Third are the Intermediaries—these are the entities like brokers and exchanges that provide the technical and operational "pipes" for the money to flow. Finally, we have the Regulators, whose job is to ensure the market develops in an orderly fashion and that everyone follows the same set of rules to protect the integrity of the system.

# Slide 5

## Legal Framework: The SCRA Definition
*   **Legal Basis:** Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956.
*   **Included Instruments:** Shares, stocks, bonds, debentures, and derivatives.
*   **Pooled Vehicles:** Units of mutual funds and collective investment schemes.
*   **Emerging Assets:** Government securities, security receipts, and Electronic Gold Receipts (EGRs).

## Notes
In India, the term "securities" isn't just a general concept; it has a very specific legal definition under the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act of 1956. This definition is quite broad to ensure investor protection across many types of assets. It covers standard items like shares and bonds, but also more complex ones like derivatives and units of mutual funds or other pooled investment vehicles. Interestingly, the definition is also dynamic; it includes government securities and has recently been updated to include things like "Electronic Gold Receipts," which allow gold to be traded as a security on an exchange.
