    Chapter 5: Economic Analysis

    Economics is the study of how individuals and societies make choices under conditions of scarcity. It begins with the assumption that people are rational actors with well-defined goals who must face trade-offs because resources—both human and material—are limited. Economic analysis is typically divided into two branches: Microeconomics (focused on individual units) and Macroeconomics (focused on the aggregate economy).

    5.1 Basic Principles of Microeconomics

    Microeconomics studies the behavior of individuals and firms in making decisions regarding the allocation of scarce resources.

    • Consumer Demand: It posits that prices and production levels are driven by what individuals choose to buy and consume.

    • Theory of the Firm: It examines how individual businesses adopt strategies related to inputs, outputs, and production levels to maximize profits.

    • Price Determination: It explains how the interaction between producers and consumers determines the prices of specific goods and services.

    5.2 Basic Principles of Macroeconomics

    Macroeconomics focuses on "the big picture" by examining factors that influence the aggregate supply and demand of an entire nation.

    • Key Focus Areas: Analysts track aggregate variables such as the Gross Domestic Product (GDP), unemployment rates, national savings, and overall inflation levels.

    • Policy Influence: Macroeconomic performance is heavily influenced by Fiscal Policy (government spending and taxes) and Monetary Policy (central bank actions regarding money supply and interest rates).

    5.3 Introduction to Various Macroeconomic Variables

    Policy makers strive for economic stability through low unemployment, price stability (low inflation), and steady growth.

    I. National Income (GDP and GNP)

    National income is the primary measure of an economy's performance.

    • Gross Domestic Product (GDP): The market value of all final goods and services produced within a country’s borders, regardless of the nationality of the producer.

    • Gross National Product (GNP): The market value of goods and services produced by a country's residents, regardless of where they are located in the world.

    • Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA): The difference between GDP and GNP ($GNP = GDP + NFIA$).

    Methods of Measuring GDP

    Method Approach Calculation Basis
    Product Method Sector-wise Output Sum of the money value of all "final goods" produced in sectors like agriculture, industry, and services.
    Income Method Aggregate Income Sum of all wages/salaries (employees), professional fees, profits (entrepreneurs), and rent/interest (investors).
    Expenditure Method Consumption Sum of private consumption, government spending, gross capital formation, and net exports.

    II. Savings and Investments

    Savings represent the portion of income not spent on current consumption.

    • Constituents: National saving is the sum of personal savings (individuals), corporate savings (undistributed profits), and public savings (government).

    • Conversion: Savings are channelled into Investments through financial instruments like equity and bonds to fund productive activities.

    III. Inflation and Interest Rates

    Inflation is the general increase in price levels leading to the erosion of the purchasing power of money.

    • Demand-Pull Inflation: Caused by demand exceeding available supply.

    • Cost-Push Inflation: Caused by an increase in the cost of inputs (raw materials, labor).

    • Measurement: Tracked via the Wholesale Price Index (WPI) and the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

    • Relationship with Rates: Higher inflation often forces central banks to raise interest rates to encourage saving and cool down consumption.

    IV. Capital Flows: FDI and FPI

    Feature Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI)
    Nature Active and stable; long-term commitment. Passive and volatile; "hot money".
    Control Investing entities participate in management decisions. Investment in markets (equity/bonds) without management involvement.
    Benefits Job creation, new technology, and managerial skills. Provides liquidity to the local capital markets.

    V. Fiscal Policy

    Fiscal policy involves government decisions regarding revenue (taxation) and expenditure (infrastructure, welfare).

    • Fiscal Deficit: Occurs when the government's expenditure exceeds its recurring revenue; it is bridged through market borrowings.

    • Current Account Deficit (CAD): The difference between recurring revenue receipts from abroad and payments made to entities abroad. A high CAD can weaken a nation's currency.

    Types of Fiscal Policy Stances

    Policy Stance Condition Action
    Expansionary Recession / Slow growth Lower taxes and higher spending to put money in the hands of citizens.
    Contractionary High Inflation / Overheating Higher taxes and lower spending to cool the economy.
    Neutral Equilibrium Income and expenditure are balanced; no major changes needed.

    VI. Monetary Policy

    Administered by the central bank (RBI in India), it manages money supply and interest rates.

    Common Monetary Policy Tools

    Tool Description
    Repo Rate The rate at which the RBI lends money to banks against securities.
    Reverse Repo The rate at which the RBI borrows money from banks against securities.
    Bank Rate The rate for long-term lending to banks without collateral.
    CRR & SLR Cash Reserve Ratio (cash held with RBI) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (liquid assets like gold/G-Secs held by banks).

    5.4 Role of Economic Analysis in Fundamental Analysis

    Economic analysis provides the context for evaluating a business's growth potential.

    • External Environment: GDP growth rates indicate the health of the broader market, while inflation and interest rates reveal the cost of capital and consumption strength.

    • Policy Room: High inflation or a large fiscal deficit can signal that the government or central bank has little room to stimulate the economy further.

    5.5 Secular, Cyclical, and Seasonal Trends

    Economic movements are categorized by their duration and predictability.

    • Secular Trends: Long-term shifts (7–10 years) often caused by technological disruptions, demographic changes, or cultural shifts (e.g., digitalization).

    • Seasonal Trends: Highly predictable annual patterns linked to weather or agriculture (e.g., higher GDP contribution from agriculture during harvest seasons).

    • Cyclical Trends: Medium-term fluctuations (2–6 years) that reverse over time.

    The Four Phases of the Economic Cycle

    1. Expansion (Boom): Characterized by high consumer confidence, increased production, and rising inflation/interest rates.

    2. Slow Down: Higher prices and interest rates begin to discourage consumption; capacity utilization rates start to fall.

    3. Recession: Declining consumption leads to layoffs and falling interest rates/inflation; businesses halt expansion.

    4. Recovery: Low inflation allows for loose monetary policy; economic activity picks up as consumers begin buying again.

    Quick Facts for Revision

    • Standard of Living: Per Capita Income (National Income / Total Population) is a better measure of standard of living than total GDP.

    • Service Sector: Constitutes approximately 60% of India's GDP at factor cost.

    • BOP Structure: The Balance of Payments now follows the IMF (BPM6) structure, dividing transactions into Current, Capital, and Financial accounts.

    • Globalization: The ability to produce and sell anything anywhere; it promotes resource efficiency but increases the risk of "contagion" from global crises.

    • Inventory Cycle: Short-term fluctuations within a commodity cycle caused by producers or customers adjusting their stock levels.

    • Key Information Sources: Major sources include government websites (RBI, SEBI, MOF), the Economic Survey, and published research reports.
