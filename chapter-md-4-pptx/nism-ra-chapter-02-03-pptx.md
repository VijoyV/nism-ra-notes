# 2.3 Structure of Securities Market

## Slide 1: The Two Pillars of Financial Markets
### Interdependence of Market Segments
*   **Segmented Structure:** The market is divided into two inseparable parts: the Primary Market and the Secondary Market.
*   **Primary Focus:** Facilitates the creation of financial assets by allowing issuers to raise fresh capital.
*   **Secondary Focus:** Facilitates the marketability and tradability of existing assets, enabling investor exit.
*   **Cyclical Relationship:** A robust secondary market promotes growth in the primary market by assuring investors of continuous liquidity.

### Notes
The securities market is not a single monolith but a dual-structured system composed of the primary and secondary markets. They are interdependent and inseparable. Think of the primary market as the "production floor" where new financial products are created and sold for the first time. The secondary market is the "resale shop" where those products are traded among investors. Without the primary market, there would be no assets to trade; without the secondary market, investors would be hesitant to buy new issues because they wouldn't have a clear way to sell them later. This synergy ensures that capital flows efficiently from those who have it to those who need it for productive use.

***

## Slide 2: Primary Market: The New Issue Engine
### Mechanisms for Raising Capital
*   **Core Function:** Used by companies (issuers) to raise fresh capital directly from the investing public or select groups.
*   **Public Offerings:** Securities are issued to the general public, allowing any eligible investor to participate.
*   **Private Placements:** Capital is raised from a select group of sophisticated investors rather than the general public.
*   **Capital Types:** Offers can include entirely new shares or an "Offer for Sale" (OFS) of existing holdings.

### Notes
The primary market, often called the "new issue market," is where the journey of a security begins. When a company needs money to build a new factory or pay off debt, it comes here to find investors. This can happen in two broad ways. A "Public Issue" is like a grand opening—anyone who meets the eligibility criteria can walk in and buy a piece of the company. A "Private Placement" is more exclusive, targeting a small group of large investors. It is important to note that sometimes a primary market event doesn't actually create new shares; instead, it might be an "Offer for Sale," where current big owners sell their old shares to the public for the first time through the primary route.

***

## Slide 3: Initial Public Offer (IPO)
### The Gateway to Public Ownership
*   **First Sale:** The very first time a company offers its common shares to the broad investing public.
*   **Growth Rationale:** Primarily used to raise equity capital to fund business expansion and future growth.
*   **Regulatory Guardrails:** SEBI defines strict eligibility criteria based on net tangible assets, profitability, and net worth.
*   **Mandatory Listing:** IPO shares must be listed on a nationwide stock exchange and offered in dematerialized form.

### Notes
An Initial Public Offer, or IPO, is one of the most significant milestones in a company’s life. It is the moment a private company "goes public," inviting the world to become its partners. The main goal is usually to get the cash needed for a massive expansion that private funding can no longer support. Because this involves inviting thousands of retail investors to take a risk, SEBI acts as a strict gatekeeper. A company can’t just launch an IPO whenever it wants; it must prove it has a track record of profits and a solid net worth. Additionally, to ensure transparency and ease of trade, these shares must be digital and listed on a major exchange immediately after the issue.

***

## Slide 4: IPO Allocation and Retail Participation
### Balancing Institutional and Individual Access
*   **Retail Reservation:** SEBI mandates that at least 35% of an eligible issue must be allocated to retail investors.
*   **Retail Definition:** Defined as individuals investing up to Rs. 2,00,000 in a single issue.
*   **Institutional Cap:** Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) can be allocated a maximum of 50% of the issue.
*   **Diversity of Base:** The remaining portion is typically allocated to non-institutional and other investor classes.

### Notes
In an IPO, the "pie" of shares is divided into specific slices to ensure fairness. SEBI wants to make sure that large institutions don't hog all the shares of a promising company. To protect the common man, the law reserves at least 35% of the shares for "Retail Investors"—these are everyday people putting in relatively smaller sums, specifically up to two lakh rupees. On the other end, professional "Qualified Institutional Buyers," like mutual funds and insurance companies, can take up to half the issue. This structure ensures a healthy mix of stable, long-term institutional money and a broad base of individual owners.

***

## Slide 5: The Anchor Investor Concept
### Signaling Quality through Commitment
*   **Definition:** A Qualified Institutional Buyer (QIB) applying for a value of Rs. 10 crore or more.
*   **Early Bidding:** Bidding for anchors opens one day before the public subscription begins.
*   **Allocation Limit:** Anchors can be allocated up to 60% of the portion reserved for QIBs.
*   **Market Sentiment:** Substantial anchor participation serves as a key indicator of the offer’s quality.

### Notes
In 2009, SEBI introduced a powerful psychological tool for the market: the "Anchor Investor." These are the big players, like global pension funds, who commit at least 10 crore rupees to an IPO before it even opens to the general public. They bid one day early, and their names and the amounts they commit are made public. Why does this matter to you as an analyst? Because it acts as a "vote of confidence." If the biggest, smartest money in the world is willing to anchor an issue, it signals that the company’s fundamentals are likely strong, providing a helpful cue for smaller investors who are still on the fence.

***

## Slide 6: Follow-on Public Offer (FPO)
### Raising Additional Capital Post-Listing
*   **Listed Issuance:** An offer of fresh securities or an offer for sale by a company already listed on an exchange.
*   **Strategic Purpose:** Used to raise capital for further growth or to restructure capital by retiring debt.
*   **Regulatory Compliance:** Often used to increase public shareholding to meet SEBI's minimum public float requirements.
*   **Market Route:** Can take the form of either fresh issue of capital or an offer for sale.

### Notes
A Follow-on Public Offer, or FPO, is what happens when a company that is already listed needs even more money. It’s like a "second round" of fundraising from the public. Companies use FPOs for various reasons: they might want to build a second factory, or they might want to pay off high-interest loans to make their balance sheet healthier. Sometimes, an FPO is done for purely regulatory reasons. If the founders still own too much of the company, SEBI might force them to sell some shares to the public to ensure the stock remains liquid and not easily manipulated.

***

## Slide 7: Private Placements and QIPs
### Targeted Issuance to Sophisticated Players
*   **Limited Audience:** Refers to issuing large quantities of shares to a select group, not exceeding 50 investors under the Companies Act.
*   **QIP Defined:** Qualified Institutional Placement is a private placement exclusively for Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs).
*   **Speed of Execution:** Offers a faster route to raise capital compared to a traditional public issue.
*   **Eligibility:** SEBI defines strict criteria for companies regarding quantum, pricing, and timing of QIPs.

### Notes
Sometimes a company needs money fast and doesn't want the months-long headache of a public IPO. In such cases, they go for a "Private Placement." This is essentially a private deal with a few big players. A very common version of this for listed companies is the "Qualified Institutional Placement" or QIP. Here, the company only talks to "sophisticated" investors like banks and mutual funds. Because these investors are experts who can do their own due diligence, the regulatory process is much faster. It’s a specialized tool for companies that have a good reputation and need to seize a sudden business opportunity.

***

## Slide 8: Preferential Issues and Rights Offers
### Strategic Allotment vs. Shareholder Protection
*   **Preferential Issue:** Issuing specified securities to a select person or group on a private placement basis.
*   **Compliance:** Issuers must follow SEBI guidelines on pricing, disclosures, and mandatory "lock-in" periods.
*   **Rights Issue:** Offering existing shareholders the chance to buy more shares in a pre-set ratio, usually at a discount.
*   **Investor Choice:** Shareholders can exercise the right, sell (renounce) the right, or let it lapse.

### Notes
When a company decides to issue more shares, it has to decide *who* gets them first. In a "Preferential Issue," the company picks a specific partner—maybe a strategic investor who can help them expand into a new country. This comes with a "lock-in" period, meaning the new partner can't just flip the shares for a quick profit. On the other hand, a "Rights Issue" is all about protecting the little guy. It gives current owners the "right" to buy new shares first, often at a cheaper price than the market. This ensures their percentage of ownership doesn't get diluted unless they choose not to participate.

***

## Slide 9: Bonus Issues and Capitalization
### Rewarding Shareholders without Cash Outflow
*   **Definition:** Additional shares issued to existing shareholders without any monetary consideration.
*   **Ratio Based:** Offered in a fixed ratio to the number of shares currently held by the investor.
*   **Source of Funds:** Can only be issued if the company has sufficient "retained earnings".
*   **Accounting Logic:** An amount equal to the value of new shares is moved from retained earnings to share capital.

### Notes
A "Bonus Issue" is often called a "stock dividend." The company gives you more shares, but you don't pay a single rupee for them. You might wonder, "where does the money come from?" It comes from the company's own past profits, known as "retained earnings." Essentially, the company is "capitalizing" its reserves—it's moving money from one pocket (savings) to another pocket (share capital) on its balance sheet. While you end up with more shares, the total value of your investment usually stays the same initially because the price of each share typically drops in proportion to the bonus ratio.

***

## Slide 10: Specialized Primary Market Transactions
### OFS, Sweat Equity, and ESOPs
*   **Offer for Sale (OFS):** Secondary market transaction done through the primary route; proceeds go to selling shareholders, not the company.
*   **Sweat Equity:** Shares issued to employees or promoters as a reward for their contribution or "sweat".
*   **ESOPs:** Options giving employees the right to buy shares at a pre-set price after a "vesting period".
*   **Agency Risk Mitigation:** Both sweat equity and ESOPs aim to align employee interests with shareholder interests.

### Notes
Beyond standard fundraising, the primary market handles specialized deals. An "Offer for Sale" or OFS is a bit of a hybrid—it looks like a primary market event, but the money doesn't go to the company; it goes to an existing big shareholder who is exiting. Then we have "Sweat Equity" and "ESOPs." These are HR tools. "Sweat Equity" rewards people for their hard work and intellectual contribution. "ESOPs" are a long-term play; they give employees a reason to stay and work hard because if the company's stock price goes up, their options become very valuable. It's a way to turn employees into owners.

***

## Slide 11: The Secondary Market Landscape
### Providing Liquidity and Price Discovery
*   **Core Function:** Facilitates the trading of already-issued securities between investors.
*   **Issuer Role:** Unlike the primary market, the issuing company is not a party to these transactions.
*   **Marketability:** Ensures that investors can liquidate their investments at any time, promoting capital formation.
*   **Market Segments:** Broadly divided into Over-the-Counter (OTC) and Exchange-Traded markets.

### Notes
Once a security is born in the primary market, it lives its life in the secondary market. This is where the action happens every day on your trading app. The key thing to remember is that when you buy a share here, the company doesn't get your money—another investor does. The secondary market is essential because it provides "liquidity." If you couldn't sell your shares easily, you probably wouldn't have bought them in the first place. This segment also performs the vital task of "price discovery," constantly updating the value of a company based on the collective wisdom of thousands of buyers and sellers.

***

## Slide 12: OTC vs. Exchange-Traded Markets
### Negotiated Trades vs. Anonymous Matching
*   **OTC Markets:** Trades are directly negotiated between two or more counterparties and settled bilaterally.
*   **Exchange-Traded:** Trading happens on a central platform (NSE/BSE) with anonymous order matching.
*   **Efficiency:** Indian exchanges use sophisticated electronic systems for high-speed execution.
*   **Settlement Guarantee:** Exchange trades are guaranteed by a clearing corporation, significantly reducing counterparty risk.

### Notes
Not all trading happens the same way. In an "Over-the-Counter" or OTC market, it's like a private negotiation—two parties agree on a price and shake hands (digitally). This is common for certain types of bonds. Most people, however, are familiar with "Exchange-Traded" markets. Here, the exchange acts like a giant, high-speed matchmaker. You don't know who you are buying from, and they don't know you. The big advantage of the exchange is the "Settlement Guarantee." If the person on the other side doesn't have the money or the shares, the exchange's clearing arm steps in to make sure you aren't cheated.

***

## Slide 13: Clearing, Settlement, and Risk Management
### Ensuring the Integrity of the Trade
*   **Clearing:** Ascertaining the net obligations (shares or money) of buyers and sellers for a period.
*   **Settlement:** The actual physical delivery of shares by the seller and payment of money by the buyer.
*   **Novation:** The Clearing Corporation acts as the buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer.
*   **Margin System:** To manage default risk, participants must pay upfront "Initial Margins" and "Mark-to-Market" (MTM) margins.

### Notes
Trading is just the first step; the real work happens behind the scenes in clearing and settlement. "Clearing" is the accounting phase where the system figures out who owes what. "Settlement" is the actual "moving day" where shares go to one account and money goes to the other. To make this 100% safe, we use a process called "Novation." The Clearing Corporation steps into the middle of every single trade. Because it takes on so much risk, it demands "Margins." You pay an "Initial Margin" as a deposit when you start a trade, and if the price moves against you, you pay "Mark-to-Market" margins to cover the potential loss. This discipline is what keeps the market from collapsing during times of high volatility.
