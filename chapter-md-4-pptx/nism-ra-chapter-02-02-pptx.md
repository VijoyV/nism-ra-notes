# 2.2 Product Definitions

## Slide 1: Diversity of Financial Securities
* **Wide Choice:** Investors select instruments based on risk appetite and return expectations.
* **Core Categories:** Broadly classified into equity, debt, and derivative securities.
* **Distinct Characteristics:** Each product has unique features defining its suitability for specific investor profiles.
* **Regulatory Oversight:** Multiple bodies like SEBI and RBI ensure orderly functioning.

### Notes
The Indian securities market offers a vast array of financial instruments, allowing investors to customize their portfolios according to their specific financial goals and tolerance for risk. These products are essentially categorized into three main buckets: equity, which represents ownership; debt, which represents a loan to an entity; and derivatives, which derive value from an underlying asset. Understanding the distinct risk-return profile of each is crucial for any analyst, as these definitions form the basis of all valuation and research activities. Regulatory oversight from institutions like SEBI and the RBI ensures that these products are issued and traded in a transparent and protected environment.,,

***

## Slide 2: Equity Shares - Fractional Ownership
* **Definition:** Units of ownership interest in a business venture.
* **Collective Ownership:** Shareholders own the company and its assets collectively.
* **Rights and Rewards:** Investors enjoy growth rewards but also bear the ultimate business risks.
* **Investor Base:** Includes individuals (Retail, HNI) and institutions (FPI, DII).

### Notes
Equity shares are the primary vehicle for ownership in the corporate world. When you buy an equity share, you are essentially buying a "fraction" of that company. This makes you a part-owner, meaning you are entitled to a share of the profits through dividends and capital appreciation as the company grows. However, this ownership comes with a catch: equity holders are the last in line during a company's liquidation. This means they bear the highest risk among all capital providers. The market for these shares is diverse, ranging from small retail investors to massive foreign portfolio investors.

***

## Slide 3: Debentures, Bonds, and Notes
* **Debt Raising:** Primary instruments used by companies and governments to borrow capital.
* **Structural Variety:** Can be secured by collateral or unsecured.
* **Interchangeable Terms:** While terms differ globally (e.g., "Gilts" in the UK), they all represent a contract of indebtedness.
* **Regulators:** Governed by the RBI, SEBI, and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.

### Notes
Debentures, bonds, and notes are the standard tools for raising debt. At its core, a debt security is a contract where an investor lends money to an issuer—like a corporation or a government—for a fixed period. In return, the issuer promises to pay a set interest rate and return the principal. While we often use these terms interchangeably, there are nuances. For instance, in the UK, government bonds are called "Gilts," while in the US, "Bonds" is the umbrella term. Analysts must always read the fine print of these instruments, as their collateral provisions and maturity dimensions can vary significantly.,,

***

## Slide 4: Classifying Debentures by Conversion
* **Fully Convertible (FCD):** Entire face value converts into ordinary shares at a pre-specified time.
* **Partly Convertible (PCD):** One portion converts to equity; the remainder stays as debt and is redeemed.
* **Non-Convertible (NCD):** Pure debt instruments with no conversion feature, repayable on maturity.
* **Quasi-Equity:** Convertible debt acts as a bridge between pure debt and ownership capital.

### Notes
Not all debt stays as debt. "Convertible" debentures offer a unique feature where they can transform into equity shares. A Fully Convertible Debenture will eventually turn entirely into ownership, while a Partly Convertible one gives the investor a mix of both worlds: some equity and some cash back at the end. Non-Convertible Debentures, or NCDs, are the simplest form—they are pure loans that must be paid back in full. These variations allow companies to manage their debt-to-equity ratios effectively while offering investors different ways to participate in a company's growth.

***

## Slide 5: Short-Term Debt Instruments
* **Maturity Limit:** Used for raising capital for periods not exceeding one year.
* **Treasury Bills (T-Bills):** Short-term debt issued by the Central Government.
* **Commercial Papers (CP):** Issued by highly credit-rated companies.
* **Certificates of Deposit (CD):** Short-term debt instruments issued by banks.

### Notes
When entities need money for a very short duration—less than a year—they turn to the "money market." The government issues Treasury Bills, which are considered the safest short-term investments. Large, financially stable companies issue Commercial Papers, and banks issue Certificates of Deposit. These instruments are vital for managing short-term liquidity and working capital needs across the economy. Because of their short duration, they are generally less sensitive to interest rate changes compared to long-term bonds.

***

## Slide 6: Domestic vs. External Bonds
* **Domestic Bonds:** Issued by local entities in the home market, denominated in home currency.
* **External Bonds:** Issued in foreign "offshore" markets in a currency other than the home currency.
* **The "Euro" Distinction:** "Euro Bonds" are issued in a currency different from the local currency of the country of issue.
* **Foreign Bonds:** Issued in a foreign country but in that country's local currency.

### Notes
Analysts must distinguish between bonds based on *where* they are issued and *which currency* they use. If an Indian company issues a Rupee bond in India, it is a domestic bond. If that same company goes to the US and issues a US Dollar bond, it is a "Foreign Bond." However, if they issue a Japanese Yen bond in the United Kingdom, it is called a "Euro Yen Bond." This naming convention is tricky—a Euro Bond doesn't have to be issued in Europe or use the Euro currency; it simply means the currency of the bond is not the local currency of the market where it’s being sold.,

***

## Slide 7: Foreign Currency Bonds and Risks
* **Issuer Preference:** Emerging market firms often issue in USD to access lower interest rates.
* **Currency Risk:** If the foreign currency appreciates, the issuer needs more domestic currency to repay.
* **Example:** Delhi International Airport Limited (DIAL) issued USD bonds in February 2020.
* **Optional Conversion:** Some foreign currency bonds can feature a conversion into equity.

### Notes
Why do companies borrow in foreign currencies? Often, it is because interest rates in developed economies like the US are much lower than in India. However, this "cheap" debt comes with a major catch: currency risk. If an Indian company borrows in Dollars and the Rupee weakens, the cost of repaying that debt in Rupee terms can skyrocket, potentially wiping out any interest rate savings. High-profile examples include major infrastructure SPVs like DIAL, which use these international markets to fund massive capital projects.,

***

## Slide 8: Masala Bonds - The INR External Bond
* **Definition:** Euro bonds denominated specifically in Indian Rupees (INR).
* **Risk Transfer:** Unlike USD bonds, Masala bonds transfer the currency risk to the foreign investor.
* **Investor Impact:** If the INR depreciates, the foreign investor receives less in their local currency.
* **Historical Note:** First issued by the International Finance Corporation in 2014 on the London Stock Exchange.

### Notes
Masala Bonds represent a unique innovation for India. They are "external" bonds, meaning they are sold to investors outside of India, but they are denominated in Rupees. This is a huge advantage for the Indian issuer because they no longer have to worry about the Rupee falling—the risk of a weak Rupee is entirely borne by the international investor. The name "Masala" was chosen to give the bond an Indian cultural identity in the global markets, similar to how China has "Dim Sum" bonds.

***

## Slide 9: Warrants
* **Rights, Not Obligations:** Gives the holder the right to buy equity at a pre-determined price.
* **Time-Bound:** Must be exercised after a specific period or they expire.
* **Issuer:** Direct issuance by companies through Stock Exchanges.
* **Regulator:** Strictly regulated by SEBI.

### Notes
Warrants are like "vouchers" for future shares. They give you the option to buy a company's stock at a fixed price, even if the market price goes much higher in the future. Because it is a "right" and not an "obligation," if the stock price stays low, you can simply let the warrant expire. They are often used as "sweeteners" when a company issues debt, giving lenders a potential slice of future equity gains.

***

## Slide 10: Market Indices - The Economic Barometers
* **Definition:** Statistical measures tracking market movement using a representative sample of shares.
* **Selection Criteria:** Based on liquidity, floating stock availability, and market capitalization.
* **Market-Cap Weighting:** Larger, more liquid companies have a higher impact on the index value.
* **Leading Examples:** NSE’s Nifty 50 (50 stocks) and S&P BSE Sensex (30 stocks).

### Notes
An index is essentially a "shortcut" to understanding how the market is doing. Instead of looking at thousands of stocks, we look at a representative sample. The Nifty 50 and the Sensex are the most famous in India. These aren't just random lists; they are carefully selected groups of the most liquid and valuable companies. Because they are "market-cap weighted," the performance of giants like Reliance or HDFC Bank has a much bigger influence on the index than smaller firms. This ensures the index truly reflects where the bulk of the market's money is moving.,

***

## Slide 11: The Vital Roles of Indices
* **Benchmarks:** Used to compare the performance of active equity funds and portfolios.
* **Market Sentiment:** Acts as a real-time indicator of investor mood.
* **Economic Barometers:** Reflect the overall health of a specific country or sector.
* **Underlying Assets:** Form the base for Index Funds and derivatives like Futures and Options.

### Notes
Indices serve several critical roles in finance. First, they are the "yardstick" for performance; if a fund manager doesn't beat the index, they haven't added much value. Second, they are "barometers"—when the Nifty is up, it signals broad economic optimism. Finally, indices are now products themselves. You can't "buy" the Nifty directly, but you can buy an "Index Fund" or trade "Index Futures" that mirror its performance exactly. This has revolutionized passive investing for millions of people.

***

## Slide 12: Mutual Fund Units
* **Definition:** Investment vehicles that pool money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio.
* **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The value of a unit, which changes daily based on the portfolio's market value.
* **Open-Ended:** Units can be bought or sold back to the fund at any time; no fixed maturity.
* **Closed-Ended:** Fixed unit capital; units are listed and traded on the stock exchange.

### Notes
Mutual funds are the "gateway" to the stock market for most retail investors. By pooling small amounts of money from thousands of people, a professional fund manager can build a highly diversified portfolio that an individual couldn't afford on their own. The NAV is the "price" of your slice of that pool. The big choice for investors is between "Open-ended" funds, which offer total liquidity through the fund house, and "Closed-ended" funds, which behave more like regular shares and must be traded on an exchange.,

***

## Slide 13: Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)
* **Hybrid Nature:** Tracks an index or commodity but trades on an exchange like a regular share.
* **Real-Time Pricing:** Unlike mutual funds (valued once a day), ETF prices fluctuate continuously.
* **Efficiency:** Typically passively managed, leading to lower expense ratios than traditional funds.
* **Structure:** Sponsored by fund houses and created through "Authorised Participants" like large banks.

### Notes
ETFs are a brilliant hybrid of a mutual fund and a stock. Like a fund, they give you instant diversification across an entire index or a commodity like gold. But like a stock, you can buy and sell them in seconds on an exchange during trading hours. Because most ETFs just "track" an index rather than having a manager try to "pick" winners, they are much cheaper to run. This cost efficiency is why ETFs have become one of the fastest-growing categories in the global financial markets.,,

***

## Slide 14: Preference Shares - The Hybrid Instrument
* **Priority Status:** Preference over common shares for dividends and capital repayment during winding up.
* **Equity Characteristics:** Holders are "owners," and payments are called "dividends" paid from post-tax profits.
* **Debt Characteristics:** Offer a pre-determined dividend rate and carry no voting rights.
* **Varieties:** Can be cumulative (unpaid dividends carry forward) or convertible into equity.

### Notes
Preference shares are the "middle child" of the financial family. They are technically equity, but they behave a lot like debt because they offer a fixed dividend. The "preference" in their name means they get paid *before* common shareholders. If a company goes bankrupt, they are also higher up the ladder for getting their money back. However, they usually don't have voting rights, meaning they don't get a say in how the company is run. They are perfect for investors who want the safety of a fixed return but with the tax status of an equity dividend.,

***

## Slide 15: Convertible Bonds - Issuer vs. Investor
* **Issuer Advantage:** Lower coupon rates because investors pay for the potential "upside" of equity.
* **Redemption Logic:** Issuers don't have to repay the debt in cash if it is converted into shares.
* **Investor Benefit:** Earn steady interest in the early stages; gain capital appreciation later.
* **Dilution Risk:** Existing shareholders' ownership percentage falls when new shares are issued on conversion.

### Notes
Convertible bonds are a strategic tool. For a company, it’s a way to borrow money at a lower interest rate because they are giving the lender a "free ticket" to become an owner later. If the company does well, the debt simply "disappears" and becomes equity, which means the company doesn't have to pay back the principal in cash. For the investor, it’s a safety net: you get interest if the company stays flat, but you get rich if the stock price soars. The only losers are current shareholders, whose "slice of the pie" gets smaller when the conversion happens.,

***

## Slide 16: Depository Receipts (ADR, GDR, IDR)
* **Definition:** Negotiable certificates representing shares of a foreign company.
* **Mechanics:** A foreign bank (depository) holds the actual shares and issues receipts in the local market.
* **ADR (American):** Non-US companies trading on US exchanges in US Dollars.
* **GDR (Global):** Traded across multiple countries, usually in the European Union.
* **IDR (Indian):** Non-Indian companies (like Standard Chartered) trading in India in Rupees.

### Notes
Depository Receipts are the "passports" for stocks. They allow you to buy shares in a foreign company without dealing with foreign currency or foreign legal systems. An American investor can buy Infosys through an ADR on the New York Stock Exchange just as easily as they buy Apple. The actual shares are locked in a vault (custodian account) in India, while the receipts trade in New York. This system has opened up global capital to Indian companies and global companies to Indian investors.,,

***

## Slide 17: Sponsored vs. Unsponsored DRs
* **Sponsored:** Initiated by the company to raise capital or increase global visibility; listed on exchanges.
* **Unsponsored:** Initiated by investors or banks without the company's direct involvement; usually OTC.
* **Two-Way Fungibility:** Allows investors to convert receipts back into original shares and vice versa.
* **Voting Rights:** Typically carry dividends but no voting rights (though SEBI is reviewing this).

### Notes
There is a big difference in how these receipts are created. If a company *wants* to be traded abroad, they "sponsor" the DR, which involves high regulatory compliance and listing on a major exchange. "Unsponsored" ones are created by third parties and are much less regulated. A key feature to remember is "two-way fungibility"—this means the market is truly integrated. If the ADR is too expensive in New York compared to the share in Mumbai, traders will convert one to the other until the prices align.,,

***

## Slide 18: Structured Debt (ELDs and CLDs)
* **Equity Linked Debentures (ELDs):** Floating rate debt with returns linked to equity indices like Nifty 50.
* **Commodity Linked (CLDs):** Returns linked to commodities, most commonly Gold and Silver.
* **Capital Protection:** Structured to return the principal at minimum, with a "slice" of market upside.
* **Credit Risk Warning:** Capital protection does *not* mean zero risk; the issuer can still default.

### Notes
Structured products like ELDs and CLDs are the "engineering" side of finance. They use a clever mix of bonds and options. Most of your money is put in a safe bond to ensure you get your "principal" back, while a small portion is used to buy options on the Nifty or Gold. If the market goes up, you get a bonus; if it goes down, you at least get your initial money back. But remember, this "protection" is only as good as the company issuing it—if the bank or firm goes bust, the protection disappears.,

***

## Slide 19: MBS and ABS - The Power of Securitization
* **Definition:** Debt instruments issued against the cash flows of other financial assets.
* **MBS:** Mortgage-Backed Securities linked specifically to home loans.
* **ABS:** Asset-Backed Securities linked to auto loans, credit card receivables, or rents.
* **Liquidity Creation:** Converts illiquid individual loans into tradable, credit-rated securities.

### Notes
Securitization is a process that turns "frozen" assets into "liquid" cash. Imagine a bank has thousands of 20-year home loans. It can't wait 20 years to get its money back to lend to new people. So, it bundles those loans together and sells them as a "security" (MBS) to investors. The investors get the interest from the homeowners, and the bank gets a lump sum of cash today. This cycle is what allows the modern financial system to provide so much credit for homes and cars.

***

## Slide 20: REITs and InvITs - Real Asset Pooling
* **REITs:** Trusts that pool money to invest in revenue-generating real estate (80% minimum).
* **InvITs:** Trusts that pool money for infrastructure projects like highways or power grids (90% minimum).
* **90% Distribution:** Mandated to pay out at least 90% of distributable cash flows to unit holders.
* **Accessibility:** Allows retail investors to "own" large-scale commercial assets with small amounts of money.

### Notes
REITs and InvITs have changed how we think about property and infrastructure. Before, if you wanted to invest in a shopping mall or a toll road, you needed hundreds of crores. Now, you can buy a "unit" for a few thousand rupees. These are designed as "income" vehicles. Because the law forces them to pay out 90% of their cash flows, they act like high-yield dividends. They are professionally managed and strictly regulated to ensure they focus on "revenue-generating" assets rather than speculative development.,

***

## Slide 21: Commodities - The Global Building Blocks
* **Definition:** Homogenous, interchangeable basic materials like metals or agricultural goods.
* **Hard Commodities:** Mined or extracted resources like crude oil, gold, and base metals.
* **Soft Commodities:** Agricultural products that are grown, such as grains and pulses.
* **Inflation Hedge:** Commodity prices and inflation are directly related, protecting real value.

### Notes
Commodities are the raw materials of civilization. The key feature of a commodity is that it is "homogenous"—one bar of 24k gold is exactly the same as another, regardless of who produced it. While "hard" commodities like oil and metals are durable, "soft" commodities like wheat have storage challenges. Investors like commodities because they act as a "hedge." When the value of paper money falls due to inflation, the price of "real" things like gold and oil usually goes up, preserving the investor's purchasing power.,

***

## Slide 22: Investing in Commodities - Modern Methods
* **Precious Metals:** Gold and silver act as long-term stores of value with low storage costs.
* **Commodity ETFs:** Funds that handle physical storage, allowing investors to trade value electronically.
* **Managed Futures:** Portfolios of futures contracts actively managed by professional managers.
* **Warehouse Receipts:** Negotiable documents proving ownership of stored goods, facilitating easy title transfer.

### Notes
You no longer need a barn or a vault to invest in commodities. Modern finance provides several ways to get exposure. Gold ETFs are the most popular, where a fund buys the physical gold and you just trade the "units" on your phone. If you are more aggressive, "Managed Futures" allow you to bet on price directions using derivatives. Even in physical trade, "Warehouse Receipts" act like paper currency for goods, allowing a farmer to sell a harvest simply by handing over a piece of paper that proves the grain is safely stored.,,
