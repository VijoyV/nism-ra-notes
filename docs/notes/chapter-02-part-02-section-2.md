Unlike equity shares, which represent **ownership**, debentures, bonds, and notes represent **borrowing**.

When an investor purchases a bond or debenture, they do **not become an owner of the business**. Instead, they become a **creditor (lender)** to the issuer.

The issuer promises:

1. Periodic interest payments (coupon)
2. Repayment of principal at maturity

Therefore, debt securities are often called **fixed-income securities**.

### Issuers

Debt securities can be issued by:

#### Companies

Examples:

* Reliance Industries
* Tata Capital
* L&T Finance

These are commonly called **Corporate Bonds** or **Debentures**.

#### Government

Examples:

* Government Securities (G-Secs)
* Treasury Bills

These are considered among the safest debt instruments because they carry sovereign backing.

#### SPVs (Special Purpose Vehicles)

A Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) is a legally separate entity created for a specific project.

Examples:

* Highway projects
* Airport projects
* Infrastructure projects

SPVs often issue bonds to finance large capital-intensive projects.

### Investors

Debt instruments are popular among:

#### Institutional Investors

* Mutual Funds
* Insurance Companies
* Pension Funds
* Banks
* FPIs

#### Individual Investors

* Retail Investors
* HNIs

Investors seeking stable income generally prefer debt instruments.

### Medium

Debt securities may be:

* Listed on stock exchanges
* Privately placed
* Traded in OTC (Over-the-Counter) markets

Listing improves transparency and liquidity.

### Regulators

## Important Debt Market Terminology

### Face Value (Par Value)

The amount that will be repaid at maturity.

Example: Bond Face Value = ₹1,000. At maturity investor receives ₹1,000.

### Coupon Rate

Interest rate promised by issuer.

Example: Face Value = ₹1,000, Coupon = 8%. Annual interest = ₹80

### Maturity Date

Date on which principal is repaid.

Example: 5-year bond issued in 2025, Maturity = 2030

### Yield

Actual return earned based on current market price.

Yield and coupon are not always the same.

This is an important NISM concept.

## Types of Debentures

| Type                         | Meaning                                                        |
| - | -- |
| **Fully Convertible (FCD)**  | Fully converted into ordinary shares; terms specified at issue |
| **Partly Convertible (PCD)** | Partly converted; non-convertible part redeemed normally       |
| **Non-Convertible (NCD)**    | Pure debt; no conversion; repayable on maturity                |


### Why Convertible Debentures Exist?

Convertible debentures combine features of:

* Debt
* Equity

Hence they are called **Hybrid Instruments**.

Investors receive:

* Fixed interest initially
* Potential ownership later

### Fully Convertible Debentures (FCD)

Entire debenture converts into equity shares.

Example: 

₹10,000 FCD

Conversion ratio:

100 shares

At conversion, debt disappears and investor becomes shareholder.

### Partly Convertible Debentures (PCD)

Part converts into equity.

Remaining portion remains debt.

Example:

₹10,000 PCD

* ₹5,000 converted to shares
* ₹5,000 redeemed on maturity

### Non-Convertible Debentures (NCD)

Pure debt instrument.

No conversion option.

Investor receives:

* Interest
* Principal repayment

NCDs are extremely common in India.

Examples:

* Tata Capital NCDs
* Bajaj Finance NCDs
* Muthoot Finance NCDs

## Short-Term Debt Instruments (≤ 1 year)

* **Treasury Bills** – issued by government
* **Commercial Papers** – issued by high-credit-rating companies
* **Certificate of Deposit** – issued by banks

### Treasury Bills (T-Bills)

Issued by: Government of India

Purpose: Meet short-term funding requirements

Maturities:

* 91 days
* 182 days
* 364 days

Important Feature:

T-Bills do not pay periodic interest.

Instead they are issued at a discount and redeemed at face value.

Example: Buy at ₹980, Receive ₹1,000 on maturity. Profit = ₹20

### Commercial Papers (CP)

Issued by: Highly rated companies

Purpose: Working capital requirements

Characteristics:

* Unsecured
* Short-term
* Lower cost than bank borrowing

Only financially strong companies can issue CPs.

### Certificate of Deposit (CD)

Issued by:

* Commercial Banks
* Financial Institutions

Purpose:

* Raise short-term deposits

Think of CDs as negotiable fixed deposits.

### Domestic Bonds

Issued:

* Inside home country
* In local currency

Example:

Reliance issues INR bonds in India.

This is a domestic bond.

### External Bonds

Issued:

* Outside home country

Often used to:

* Access global investors
* Obtain lower borrowing costs
* Diversify funding sources


## External Bonds Subclassification

* **Foreign Bond**: issued in foreign country, denominated in that country's local currency
* **Euro Bond**: issued in foreign country, denominated in a currency NOT local to that country

### 2.2.2.1 Foreign Bonds (Detailed)

| Aspect    | Details                                   |
|  | -- |
| Issuers   | Companies, Government, SPVs               |
| Investors | Institutional and Individual              |
| Medium    | Direct issuance through Stock Exchanges   |
| Regulator | Regulators in respective country of issue |


#### Key Features

* Issued outside issuer's home country.
* Denominated in the currency of the country where issued.
* Governed by local regulations of that country.

Example:

An Indian company issues USD bonds in the United States.

This is a foreign bond.

#### Risk for Issuer

This is the most important exam point. The issuer bears currency risk.

Example: 

Borrowing: USD 100 million with Exchange rate: ₹80/USD

Repayment obligation: ₹8,000 million.

If USD rises to ₹90, Repayment becomes: ₹9,000 million

Even though debt amount remains unchanged in USD, the repayment burden increases substantially.

#### Why Companies Issue Foreign Bonds

* Lower interest rates
* Larger investor base
* Better liquidity
* Diversified funding sources

### 2.2.2.2 Euro Bonds / External Bonds

| Aspect    | Details                                   |
|  | -- |
| Issuers   | Companies, Government, SPVs               |
| Investors | Institutional and Individual              |
| Medium    | Direct issuance through Stock Exchanges   |
| Regulator | Regulators in respective country of issue |


#### Key Features

A Euro Bond is:

* Issued outside issuer's home country
* Denominated in a currency different from the local currency of the country where issued

Example:

US Dollar bond issued in Kuwait.

* Country of issue = Kuwait
* Local currency = Kuwaiti Dinar
* Bond currency = USD

Hence it becomes a Euro Bond.

> Note: "Euro Bond" does **not** necessarily mean Europe or Euro currency. It simply refers to offshore issuance in a non-local currency.

### 2.2.2.3 Masala Bonds (Special Category)

* Euro bonds denominated in Indian Rupees (INR)
* Issued outside India
* First issued by International Finance Corporation (IFC) in 2014
* Popular overseas listing venue: London Stock Exchange

#### Why Masala Bonds Are Important

They shift currency risk from issuer to investor. This makes them attractive for Indian issuers.

#### Key Difference from Foreign Bonds

|                      | Foreign Bonds                   | Masala Bonds (Euro Bonds)                            |
| -- | - | - |
| Currency risk bearer | Issuer                          | Investor                                             |
| Reason               | If foreign currency appreciates | If INR depreciates against investor's local currency |


## Equity vs Debt

| Aspect                      | Equity Shares             | Debentures/Bonds                  |
|  | - |  |
| Relationship                | Owner                     | Lender                            |
| Return                      | Dividend (not guaranteed) | Interest (contractual obligation) |
| Voting Rights               | Usually available         | Not available                     |
| Repayment                   | No maturity               | Fixed maturity                    |
| Risk                        | Higher                    | Generally lower                   |
| Priority during liquidation | Last                      | Higher than shareholders          |
